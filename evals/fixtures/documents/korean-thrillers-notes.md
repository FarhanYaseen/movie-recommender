# Notes on two Korean thrillers

Personal notes comparing Parasite and Oldboy, written for a film-club
discussion on class and revenge in Korean cinema.

## Parasite: architecture as class diagram

Bong Joon-ho stages Parasite vertically. The Kim family lives in a
semi-basement where drunks urinate at the window; the Park family lives up a
hill, up a driveway, up more stairs. Every journey between households is a
climb or a descent, and the film's turning point is literally a flood flowing
downhill into the poor neighborhood. The famous peach-fuzz allergy scheme is
comedy right up until the basement door opens, and then the same staircase
geometry turns the satire into horror. The smell motif — the Parks wrinkling
their noses at a scent they cannot name — does more class analysis than any
speech.

## Oldboy: revenge as a sealed room

Park Chan-wook's Oldboy opens with fifteen years of unexplained imprisonment
in a private cell that looks like a cheap hotel room, and the film never
really lets Dae-su out of it. The five-day deadline to discover why he was
held, the single-take corridor fight with a hammer, the live octopus scene —
all of it circles one idea: the revenger and his target are locked in the same
room, and the key was thrown away long before the movie started. The ending's
hypnotist offers forgetting as the only exit, which is crueler than any
punishment.

## Common thread

Both films trap families inside structures someone else designed — a house, a
cell — and let the architecture do the violence.
