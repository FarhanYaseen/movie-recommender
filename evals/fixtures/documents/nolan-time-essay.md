# Christopher Nolan and the architecture of time

These viewing notes examine how Christopher Nolan treats time as a physical
material rather than a backdrop. They were written as a personal essay after a
rewatch of his science-fiction work.

## Inception and nested tempo

In Inception, each dream level runs at its own clock speed. A van falling from
a bridge takes seconds at street level, minutes one layer down, and hours in
the snow fortress. The film's famous spinning top is less important than this
tempo structure: suspense comes from synchronized deadlines across layers, the
musical cue acting as a metronome that every level can hear. The heist plot is
really a scheduling problem, and the emotional payload — Cobb's guilt about
Mal — hides inside the mechanism like a stowaway.

## Interstellar and time as a resource

Interstellar literalizes relativity. One hour on Miller's planet costs seven
years of Murph's life on Earth, so every minute spent near the black hole
Gargantua is billed in missed birthdays. The film's most devastating scene is
not an action sequence but a backlog of video messages, decades of a
daughter's life arriving at once. Where Inception nests time, Interstellar
spends it; love is framed, controversially, as the one signal that crosses the
bookshelf between dimensions.

## A shared grammar

Across both films the pattern repeats: establish an exchange rate between
clocks, then make the audience feel the cost of the conversion. Nolan's
time machines are never machines at all — they are budgets.
